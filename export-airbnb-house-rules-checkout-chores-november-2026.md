## Cuerpo (markdown convertido desde el HTML guardado en KV)

*Published October 22, 2026, based on Airbnb's own policy pages as of October 21. Airbnb can clarify details before November 20, so re-check its pages before you change your listing.*



For a lot of hosts, the checkout instructions have quietly been part of the cleaning budget: strip the beds, start the towels, wash your dishes, take the trash out. Airbnb's new house rules policy says hosts generally can't require that kind of work for bookings made on or after **November 20, 2026**.

The work doesn't disappear. It moves, and in a lot of properties it lands on whoever turns the home over. That makes this a scope-of-work problem, not just a listing-settings problem.

## What changes, and when

- **Booking date controls.** The policy applies to reservations booked on or after November 20, 2026. Airbnb says the changes won't affect pending or confirmed reservations, so a stay booked in October keeps the rules that were on the listing when the guest booked. Don't read it as "everything checking out after November 20."
- **The editor is already live.** Airbnb announced the change on October 20 and the new house rules and requests section is in the listing editor now, so you can fix things before the policy takes effect. The old standalone checkout instructions tab no longer appears separately.
- **Seven core house rules.** Maximum overnight guests (new), quiet hours, events, pets, smoking and vaping, commercial photography and filming, and lock up at checkout (new).
- **Everything else is a request.** Guests must follow house rules. They're only encouraged to follow requests, and requests have to be reasonable.

## What you can and can't ask guests to do

| Task | Can still ask (as a request) | Can't require |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dishes | Put used dishes in the sink | Wash and put away dishes |
| Laundry | Gather used towels | Strip the bed or start a load of laundry |
| Trash | Sort trash and recycling into indoor bins | Take garbage off-site |
| Air conditioning | Turn it off when leaving | Avoid using an advertised feature during the stay |

Airbnb's general test is that hosts can't require tasks that "require significant effort or time, or would typically be performed by a cleaner." Two things to know about it:

- **There's no published minute count or task limit.** Airbnb says reasonableness depends on the listing type, the stay, and local factors. Its examples of context exceptions are animal-proof trash containers in bear country and shower limits during drought restrictions.
- **Wiping surfaces and similar work aren't named.** Treating routine surface cleaning as cleaner work is a reasonable inference, not Airbnb's wording.

## Existing bookings, messages, signs and house manuals

Airbnb's policy says hosts "may not communicate new house rules or requests in other sections of their listing page or after booking, including by message, signs in the home, or in person."

The key word is *new*. Airbnb says friendly reminders of rules you already disclosed are still fine.

Where it gets less clear: Airbnb's text names messages, signs and in-person instructions. It doesn't name house manuals or digital guidebooks. Some summaries go further than the official wording. Because "including" introduces examples rather than a closed list, the safer reading is that a guidebook shouldn't be where a brand-new obligation first appears, especially since Airbnb's help center says guests see the house manual after the booking is confirmed. Equipment instructions ("here's how the thermostat works") are a different thing from obligations ("strip the bed before you leave"). Compare each guidebook entry against what your listing disclosed before booking.

## San Antonio hosts: keep your required City notices

San Antonio's STR ordinance (Chapter 16, Article XXII, adopted June 13, 2024 and linked from the Development Services STR page) requires certain information to be posted at the property, such as occupancy, quiet hours, a 24-hour contact, trash instructions and emergency or evacuation information, plus the permit number in advertisements.

Airbnb's rule is aimed at new house rules and requests added outside the designated listing sections or after booking. It isn't a ban on legally required notices. But neither Airbnb nor the City has published guidance on how the two fit together, so a City-required notice that adds an obligation not disclosed in your listing is a gray area. Our read of the safe approach:

- Don't take down safety, permit or occupancy notices the City requires.
- Put the local obligations that apply to guests in the listing's designated rules and local-rules sections before booking.
- If Airbnb flags something, save the text and a screenshot, then ask Airbnb and San Antonio Development Services how it should be handled.
- If you have an HOA, check it too. Airbnb allows contracts and notices tied to documented community-association rules, and the City says it doesn't enforce HOA covenants.

This is not legal advice. For permit and compliance questions specific to your property, confirm with Development Services or an attorney.

## Contracts, ID checks and apps (the host-side rules)

These mostly affect hosts and property managers rather than cleaners, but they're part of the same change:

- **Contracts:** Airbnb says it doesn't require guests to sign host contracts and doesn't help enforce them. Separate contracts are allowed only for limited reasons: legal requirements, community association rules, an optional amenity (a golf cart, say), monthly stays of 28 nights or more, and Airbnb Luxe listings.
- **ID and apps:** Hosts may physically check a guest's ID in person at check-in if they disclose it in the requests section, but generally may not keep a copy. Requiring a third-party app or registration on another website is restricted, with exceptions. Keyless-entry apps are allowed if guests can request another way in.

## Fines: don't rename a penalty

Airbnb's policy says hosts may not "impose fines or other penalties on guests." The exception is notice: if a community association or local government might fine the property, you can say so in the designated section of the listing.

That doesn't mean damage goes uncompensated. It means a claim has to follow Airbnb's terms and evidence, not an automatic fee schedule. Renaming a banned $100 fine as a "damage fee" doesn't solve it. This is where factual documentation matters. At Chabod, a turnover that finds damage or an unusual mess gets photographed, noted and reported to the host. We document what we find and leave the penalty question out of it.

## What this means for your turnover scope

Here's how the removed guest chores translate into scope decisions. The right-hand column is our suggestion, not Airbnb guidance:

| Task | Guests can be asked to | Your turnover scope should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bed stripping | Nothing (can't require it) | Who strips, and that linens are inspected as they come off |
| Laundry | Gather used towels | Who starts, carries and returns the load, per your linen plan |
| Dishes | Put used dishes in the sink | How leftover food, dish handling and the dishwasher cycle are handled |
| Trash | Sort into indoor bins | Who runs trash to the outdoor bin, and who is authorized to dispose of it |
| Towels | Gather them (request only) | Pickup even if the guest didn't gather them |
| Damage or unusual mess | Nothing | Photos, notes and a report to the host, no automatic charge |

**How much time does this add?** Nobody has measured it yet. We found no credible before-and-after data on added turnover time, and the "dishes take 60 to 90 minutes" claims in forum posts are one person's estimate. The useful move is to track your own: tasks, load counts and dish condition across a few real turnovers before you decide whether your scope or price should change.

**If you do decide to adjust your cleaning fee:** Airbnb's service fee is a percentage of the nightly price and host-charged fees. For most hosts on the single-fee model it's 15.5%. That means a cleaning-fee increase doesn't reach you in full. Raising the fee by $20 nets about $16.90 before your other costs. To net a full $20, the gross increase would be about $23.67 ($20 ÷ 0.845). Whether to change your fee at all is your call, Airbnb doesn't say hosts have to, and you shouldn't change the terms of a reservation that's already confirmed.

## Flags, appeals and what's still unclear

The editor highlights text that doesn't comply, explains why, and sends you an email with tips. You get an opportunity to appeal. If a problem isn't resolved, guests may see an alert such as "includes cleaning requests." Persistent violations may lead to a listing notice, reduced search visibility or other action under Airbnb's Terms of Service.

Still unclear as of today:

- How the appeal works step by step, and how fast Airbnb responds.
- How Airbnb decides what's "reasonable," beyond listing type, stay type and local factors.
- Whether loading and starting a dishwasher counts as "significant effort."
- Whether a local gap, such as no recycling pickup, earns an exception.
- Whether your channel manager or property management software overwrites fixes you make directly in Airbnb. Check yours after every sync.
- Any real example of enforcement. We found none yet, since the policy hasn't taken effect.

Early host discussion online is lively but mixed. Two r/airbnb_hosts threads have drawn more than 1,100 comments combined (comment counts, not unique hosts). The themes are dishes, shared homes, trash between stays and how requests can be enforced, with reactions from relief to frustration. It isn't a survey of San Antonio hosts, and it doesn't support "everyone is furious" or "everyone is thrilled." Local groups such as the STR Association of San Antonio have shared summaries with hosts, and Airbnb's own pages remain the authority.

## Your checklist before November 20

Items marked *Stated by Airbnb* come from Airbnb's pages. *Reasonable inference* items are our suggestions.

- **Save today's listing house rules and checkout instructions** so you can show what was in place for each booking date. *(Reasonable inference)*
- **Move legitimate requests into the Requests section** and make sure the seven core rules match your permit and actual limits. *(Stated by Airbnb)*
- **Remove required chores:** strip beds, start laundry, wash dishes, take trash off-site. *(Stated by Airbnb)*
- **Check scheduled messages, digital guidebooks, QR-code links and printed signs** so none add new obligations that weren't in the listing at booking. *(Messages and signs: stated by Airbnb. Guidebooks: reasonable inference.)*
- **Keep City-required notices** and confirm anything flagged with Airbnb and Development Services. *(Reasonable inference)*
- **Check advertised features and fees:** no restricted access, and any mandatory fee for an advertised feature in the price shown at booking. Remove automatic fines. *(Stated by Airbnb)*
- **Inventory contracts, ID collection and third-party check-in apps** against the exceptions above. *(Stated by Airbnb)*
- **Update your cleaner's written scope** to cover everything that used to be a guest chore. *(Reasonable inference)*
- **Keep separate rule versions per platform.** We didn't verify equivalent chore limits on Vrbo or Booking.com, so don't assume Airbnb's text applies there. *(Reasonable inference)*

A suggested timeline, which is our suggestion and not an Airbnb deadline: audit the listing and save snapshots by October 27, hold the host-and-cleaner scope meeting by November 6, re-check Airbnb's pages around November 13 for clarifications, take a final snapshot and test the guest view on November 19, then track real task time and any support outcomes after November 20.

## The real problem is scope, not chores

When checkout chores were guest tasks, the scope lived in a house manual nobody had to sign off on. Now it has to live in the scope of work: who strips the beds, who starts the laundry, who takes the trash out, what gets photographed when a guest leaves a mess, and who gets told.

That's why we don't hand clients a contract. We write down a scope of work, and when something in it changes, the property's turnover procedure changes with it, and our crews train on the update. Most turnover surprises aren't a work-ethic problem. They come from a task nobody wrote down, or an expectation that sat in someone's head.

This is also separate from Airbnb's host fee change and from [San Antonio's STR permit fee increase](/blog/san-antonio-str-permit-fee-increase-october-2026). Those are different decisions by different organizations. They overlap in timing, not in cause.
